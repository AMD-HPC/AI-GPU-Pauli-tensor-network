\begin{abstract}
Existing multi-product formula (MPF) error mitigation for quantum
lattice simulations operates on the autocorrelation $C(t) = \langle
O(t)O(0)\rangle$: that is, a single scalar per cycle.  We propose
using the non-identity Pauli weight $n(v,t)$, defined as the probability
that the operator has support at site $v$ at cycle $t$, as a space-time
observable.  The $n(v,t)$ directly reveals how noise deforms the
operator lightcone across the lattice and especially at the frontier.

We introduce the \emph{wavemap} based on the lightcone frontier: a
spatial portrait of noise effects assigning each site a
per-noise-level arrival delay $l_\gamma(v)$ and cross-entropy loss
$\mathcal{L}_\gamma(v) = \sum_t -\ln(n_\gamma/n_{\rm nl})$ in a
delay-adaptive time window (i.e., information loss).  These
observables are exact at the wavefront (bond dimension $\chi\approx1$,
no truncation error). The frontier is precisely where classical
simulation is most faithful and where $C(t)$-based MPF is least
informative.

To characterize the noise, eigenvalue analysis of the composed
gate-plus-noise Pauli transfer matrices shows that for pure
amplitude damping ($<0.1\%$ off-diagonal contribution) the noise
rescales the PTM eigenvalue magnitudes without rotating eigenvectors,
leaving the spatial propagation pattern entirely determined by the
gate.  The wavefront therefore provides a direct, model-free tool to
visualize and quantify the nature of the noise — damping shifts the
wavefront later and fainter, while rotation-type noise produces a
qualitatively different signature of premature weight injection,
making the frontier appear faster at affected sites.

Introducing cross-entropy loss as the MPF objective, subject to the
Lieb-Robinson causal constraint $n_{\rm MPF} \leq n_{\rm nl}$, we
recover $\sim$25\% more information than the best individual noisy sample on
a uniform-noise rectangular lattice.  On an IBM heavy-hex lattice with
heterogeneous hardware noise, the method identifies an
information-starved regime where the causal constraint is already
saturated — pointing to calibrated uniform synthetic noise as the next
required experiment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a local operator evolves under a noisy Hamiltonian, its Pauli weight
$n(v,t)$ spreads outward from the initialization site as a wave.
Compared to the noiseless reference, the noisy wave travels slower, reaches
fewer sites, and arrives with less amplitude.
This is not a model prediction — it is a direct observation from the data,
visible without fitting or assumptions, see Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap}.
A different noise regime, with off-diagonal Pauli transfer matrix terms,
can inject weight at unvisited sites, making parts of the frontier appear
faster rather than slower — a qualitatively distinct signature.

\centering

Color encodes $\log_{10} n(v,t)$; white = below threshold.
The wavefront advances diagonally in the (space, time) plane at velocity $v_{\rm hop}$.
Noise suppresses amplitudes, pushing the threshold-crossing later, a uniform

\label{fig:heatmap}
\end{figure}


This observation motivates the central question of this paper:
can we quantify, site by site and cycle by cycle, how much information
the noisy evolution has lost relative to the noiseless reference?

The standard MPF approach~\cite{mpf-ref} fits the autocorrelation
$C(t) = \langle Z_c(t) Z_c(0)\rangle$ across noise levels $\gamma$
and extrapolates to zero noise.
This is a single scalar per cycle, compressing the entire space-time evolution
into one number at each $t$.
Two limitations follow.
First, spatial information is lost: sites strongly affected by noise and sites
barely affected both contribute to the same scalar $C(t)$.
Second, $C(t)$ is dominated by contributions from the interior of the
lightcone, where bond dimension $\chi$ is large and belief propagation is
approximate. The wavefront, where $\chi\approx1$ and the simulation is exact,
contributes least to $C(t)$.

The non-identity Pauli weight $n(v,t)$ resolves both limitations.
Any operator $O_c(t)$ can be expanded in the $n$-qubit Pauli basis
$\{P\} = \{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}$ as $O_c(t) = \sum_P c_P(t)\, P$,
where the real coefficients $c_P(t)$ satisfy $\sum_P c_P(t)^2 = 1$.
Then $n(v,t)$ is defined as the total weight on Paulis that act
non-trivially at site $v$:
\begin{equation}
    n(v,t) = \sum_{\substack{P:\, P_v \neq I}} c_P(t)^2
    \label{eq:nvt}
\end{equation}
By normalization, $n(v,t) \in [0,1]$ is the probability that a Pauli
string sampled from the operator distribution acts non-trivially at site $v$~\cite{nahum2018operator,Tan2025Operator}.
It is a probability distribution over the lattice, evolving in time as the
operator spreads. At the wavefront it is computed exactly at any $\chi$:
frontier sites carry only nascent correlations and $\chi\approx1$ by
construction. This is where the simulation is most faithful and where noise
effects are most cleanly measurable.

The \emph{frontier} at cycle $t$ is the set of sites that first acquire
significant operator weight at that cycle:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}(t) = \bigl\{ v : n(v,t) > \varepsilon_{\rm th}
                             \text{ and } n(v,t-1) \leq \varepsilon_{\rm th} \bigr\}
    \label{eq:frontier}
\end{equation}
The frontier is the leading edge of the lightcone, where $\chi\approx1$ and
the simulation is exact. For each site $v$ and noise level $\gamma$, we
define the \emph{arrival delay} $l_\gamma(v) = t_\gamma(v) - t_{\rm nl}(v)$
as the number of cycles by which the noisy frontier lags the noiseless one,
and the \emph{cross-entropy loss}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\gamma(v) = \sum_{t} -\ln\frac{n_\gamma(v,t)}{n_{\rm nl}(v,t)}
    \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
summed over a delay-adaptive window centered on arrival, as the accumulated
information loss at site $v$.
Together, $l_\gamma(v)$ and $\mathcal{L}_\gamma(v)$ form the \emph{wavemap},
a spatial portrait of noise effects across the lattice, and the
space-time trajectory of the frontier $\mathcal{F}(t)$ connects the
per-site observables to the global propagation dynamics.

Several lines of prior work inform this paper.
Operator spreading in random unitary circuits~\cite{nahum2018operator,Tan2025Operator}
established the butterfly velocity and domain-wall width as natural observables
for wavefront characterization; our work applies analogous ideas to structured
Hamiltonian evolution with noise amplification.
Real-time operator evolution in two and three dimensions via sparse Pauli
dynamics~\cite{Begusic2025RealTime} and Pauli propagation under arbitrary local
noise~\cite{rudolph2025simulating} provide the simulation framework on which
CppSim is based.
Noise characterization from circuit measurements using sparse Pauli-Lindblad
models~\cite{Hu2025Demonstration} and self-consistent Pauli noise
learning~\cite{Chen2026Efficient} are complementary approaches that infer noise
rates by fitting a model; our approach uses the spatial wavefront pattern to
characterize noise without model assumptions.
The multi-product formula for error mitigation has been applied to autocorrelation
functions in tensor network simulations of the heavy-hex
lattice~\cite{robertson2025lmpf,dalberto2026cppsim}.
Our work extends these in two directions: replacing the scalar autocorrelation
with the full space-time Pauli weight field $n(v,t)$, and replacing squared
error with cross-entropy loss as the MPF objective.

We show via PTM eigenvalue analysis that the noise models studied here are
pure amplitude damping, leaving the spatial propagation pattern determined
by the gate.
The Lieb-Robinson bound~\cite{liebrobinson1972} states that information
cannot propagate faster than a finite velocity in any local lattice Hamiltonian,
implying $n_\gamma(v,t) \leq n_{\rm nl}(v,t)$ for pure damping noise —
the noiseless evolution is the causal ceiling.
Entropy-optimal MPF with this constraint $n_{\rm MPF} \leq n_{\rm nl}$
recovers $\sim$25\% more information than the best noisy sample on a uniform-noise
rectangular lattice.
On the IBM heavy-hex lattice with heterogeneous hardware noise, the method
identifies an information-starved regime where the causal constraint is
already saturated, pointing to calibrated uniform synthetic noise as the
next required experiment.

\section{Hamiltonian, Noise, and Eigenvalue Analysis}
\label{sec:setup}

We simulate the kicked isotropic Heisenberg model in the Heisenberg picture:
\begin{equation}
    H = J \sum_{\langle ij\rangle} \bigl(X_iX_j + Y_iY_j + Z_iZ_j\bigr),
    \qquad
    U_{\rm kick} = \prod_v R_x(2\phi)_v,\quad \phi = 1.41371669
\end{equation}
with per-bond coupling $J_{\langle ij\rangle}$ from device characterization
and Trotter step $\varepsilon = 0.10$.
Each Trotter cycle applies the bond gates across all color classes in sequence,
followed by a single kick gate $R_x(2\phi)$ at every site.
We use CppSim~\cite{dalberto2026cppsim}, a GPU-accelerated belief propagation
tensor network simulator in which operators are evolved via $16\times16$ real
Pauli transfer matrices (PTMs), with bond dimension $\chi$ controlled by
Householder QR truncation at cutoff $\delta$.
All results use $\varepsilon=0.10$, $\delta=10^{-3}$, output precision $10^{-7}$,
and threshold $\varepsilon_{\rm th}=10^{-4}$.

Two implementation choices are worth stating explicitly.
First, the sub-gates within each bond ($G_1\ldots G_4$ and interleaved noise
channels) are pre-composed into a single $16\times16$ PTM per bond before
simulation; belief propagation and truncation are applied once per full Trotter
cycle, after all color classes have been applied.
This differs from implementations that truncate after each color class or
sub-gate~\cite{dalberto2026cppsim}, producing slightly faster $\chi$ growth
per cycle, but the composed and sequential forms are physically equivalent.
Second, $n(v,t)$ is recorded once per full Trotter cycle, after the kick gate
fires — it is a snapshot of the operator state at the end of each complete cycle.
The wavefront and all wavemap observables are defined in terms of these
end-of-cycle snapshots.
A hardware experiment or a simulation that measures mid-cycle would observe
a different wavefront trajectory; our observables are specific to the
post-kick measurement point.

We study two topologies under two noise models.
For the IBM heavy-hex $3\times3$ lattice (68 sites, 76 bonds), per-bond
noise PTMs measured from device characterization (ibm\_basquecountry) are
interleaved with the gate decomposition:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PTM}_{\rm noisy}^{(b)} =
    G_1 \cdot M_\gamma^{(b)} \cdot G_2(\alpha_b) \cdot M_\gamma^{(b)}
    \cdot G_3(\alpha_b) \cdot M_\gamma^{(b)} \cdot G_4(\delta_b)
    \label{eq:noisy_ptm}
\end{equation}
where $M_\gamma^{(b)} = \gamma M_{\rm hw}^{(b)} + (1-\gamma)I$ amplifies
the hardware noise channel by factor $\gamma$, and $G_1\ldots G_4$ implement
the hardware gate decomposition.
For the rectangular $7\times7$ lattice (49 sites, 84 bonds), we use synthetic
depolarizing noise: rows $1\ldots15$ of the ideal bond PTM $G^{(b)}$ are
scaled by $(1-p\gamma)$ with $p=0.01$, uniformly damping all non-identity
output Pauli components.

Before studying the wavemap observables, we establish the noise character
directly from the composed PTMs.

Noise adds no rotation structure — it only scales eigenvalue magnitudes,
leaving all Pauli mixing to the gate $G$.
The noise channel is therefore a pure damping operator at the PTM level:
it delays the wavefront by reducing amplitudes below threshold without
redirecting the spatial propagation pattern, which remains entirely
determined by the Hamiltonian.

\section{The Pauli Lightcone and Wavefront}
\label{sec:observables}

For each site $v$ and noise level $\gamma$, the \emph{arrival time}
$t_\gamma(v) = \min\{t : n_\gamma(v,t) > \varepsilon_{\rm th}\}$
is the first cycle at which the wave crosses threshold,
and the \emph{arrival delay}
\begin{equation}
    l_\gamma(v) = t_\gamma(v) - t_{\rm nl}(v)
    \label{eq:delay}
\end{equation}
is the number of cycles by which the noisy wave lags the noiseless one.
For the noise models studied here (pure amplitude damping, Section~\ref{sec:setup}),
$n_\gamma(v,t) \leq n_{\rm nl}(v,t)$ at every site and cycle, so $l_\gamma(v) \geq 0$ always.%
\footnote{Rotation-type noise, with off-diagonal PTM terms injecting weight at
unvisited sites, can make the noisy wave arrive \emph{before} the noiseless at
isolated sites, giving $l_\gamma(v) < 0$. The Lieb-Robinson bound still holds
for the noiseless evolution, bounding its propagation speed; what can be inverted
is the site-by-site ordering between noisy and noiseless, since rotation noise
injects weight independently of the causal structure of the Hamiltonian.
In that regime the wavemap requires a signed delay.}

The ratio $r_\gamma(v) = n_\gamma(v, t_\gamma(v)) / n_{\rm nl}(v, t_\gamma(v))$
compares both waves at the moment of noisy arrival.
At that cycle the noisy wave has just crossed threshold
($n_\gamma \approx \varepsilon_{\rm th}$), while the noiseless wave has been
growing at $v$ for $l_\gamma(v)$ extra cycles
($n_{\rm nl} \gg \varepsilon_{\rm th}$).
The ratio $r_\gamma(v) \leq 1$ measures the combined effect of cumulative
path-dependent damping and the head start the noiseless wave gained during
the delay. It is bounded: $r_\gamma(v) \to 1$ when both waves arrive
simultaneously ($l_\gamma(v) = 0$) and decreases as the delay grows.

The ratio $r_\gamma(v)$ is a first step toward a probability comparison:
since $n(v,t)$ is a probability (Eq.~\ref{eq:nvt}), the ratio
$n_\gamma(v,t)/n_{\rm nl}(v,t)$ measures how much of the noiseless
probability at site $v$ and cycle $t$ the noisy evolution retains.
The natural information-theoretic measure of this loss is the
cross-entropy, which we sum over a delay-adaptive window
$[t_\gamma(v) - \Delta,\, t_\gamma(v) + \Delta]$ with
$\Delta = \max(1, l_\gamma(v))$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\gamma(v) = \sum_{t \in \text{window}} -\ln\frac{n_\gamma(v,t)}{n_{\rm nl}(v,t)}
    \label{eq:loss2}
\end{equation}
This is the accumulated information loss at site $v$: zero when the two
distributions coincide, positive when noise suppresses the probability,
and with window width set by the delay itself so no free parameter is introduced.

The delay field $l_\gamma(v)$, the ratio $r_\gamma(v)$, the entropy loss
(Figure~\ref{fig:wavemap}),
$\mathcal{L}_\gamma(v)$, and the space-time frontier $\mathcal{F}(t)$
together form the \emph{wavemap}: a spatial portrait of how noise deforms
the Pauli lightcone, site by site.
All wavemap observables are exact at any bond dimension $\chi$, since frontier
sites have $\chi\approx 1$ by construction, and all are causally bounded
by the Lieb-Robinson structure.
In the following section we show how these observables can be refined through
the multi-product formula: fitting the wavemap across noise levels $\gamma$
and extrapolating toward the noiseless reference, with the Lieb-Robinson bound
as a causal constraint, yields a spatially-resolved error correction of the
operator lightcone.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We present results in three stages, each building on the previous.
First, we characterize the global wavefront: the frontier velocity $v_{\rm hop}$
and per-site delay distribution $l_\gamma(v)$ establish how noise slows the
wave and which sites it fails to reach.
Second, we introduce the cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}_\gamma(v)$ as a
per-site information measure and show how its spatial and per-shell distributions
reveal the structure of noise across the lattice.
Third, we apply the multi-product formula to the wavemap in two forms:
a frontier-position MPF that recovers the noiseless wavefront trajectory,
and an entropy-optimal MPF that directly minimizes information loss subject
to the Lieb-Robinson causal constraint, demonstrating measurable information
recovery on the uniform-noise topology.

The delay distribution has a natural spatial structure:
sites close to the center (small BFS distance $d$) have crossed fewer bonds
and accumulated less damping, so $l_\gamma(v)$ tends to be small.
Sites at the frontier of the noiseless lightcone have large $l_\gamma(v)$
— or do not appear in the statistics at all (the noisy wave never arrives).

The \emph{survivorship bias} in $\mu_l$ must be read carefully:
a smaller mean delay does not necessarily mean a faster noisy wave.
It may mean the slow distant sites dropped out of the sample entirely.
The correct summary is the pair $(n_{\rm reached},\, \mu_l)$ together —
or the full per-frontier-shell distribution.

The ratio $r_\gamma(v)$ is bounded in $[0,1]$ for all observed sites,
confirming the causal structure: the noiseless wave always outgrows the
noisy wave at $v$ during the delay period.

The ratio encodes the cumulative damping along the propagation path:
for uniform depolarizing noise with $N$ gate crossings,
$r_\gamma(v) \approx (1-p\gamma)^N / g(l_\gamma(v))$
where $g(l)$ is the noiseless wave growth factor over $l$ cycles at site $v$.
Sites with the same delay $l_\gamma(v)$ but different $N$ will have different
ratios — the ratio carries path-length information independent of the delay.

\textbf{Rect $7\times7$}: $\mu_r \approx 0.42$--$0.50$ with $\sigma_r/\mu_r \approx 0.65$.
The large coefficient of variation reflects heterogeneous path lengths $N(v)$
across frontier sites on the 4-color lattice — sites at the same BFS distance
may have been touched by 1, 2, or 3 gate layers depending on their position.

\textbf{Heavy-hex}: $\mu_r \approx 0.72$--$0.83$ with $\sigma_r/\mu_r \approx 0.40$.
The smaller coefficient of variation reflects more uniform propagation paths on the degree-2/3 graph.
The non-monotone $\gamma$ dependence ($\mu_r$ larger at $\gamma=3$ than $\gamma=2$)
mirrors the non-monotone delay ($\mu_l$ smaller at $\gamma=3$): fewer sites
with shorter paths survive in the $\gamma=3$ sample, giving a higher ratio.

\subsection{Lieb-Robinson-constrained spatial MPF}
\label{sec:mpf}

The noiseless frontier $F_{\rm nl}(t) = \max_{v\in\mathcal{F}_{\rm nl}(t)} d(v,c)$
is the Lieb-Robinson ceiling for operator spreading.
We define the MPF-corrected frontier:
\begin{equation}
    F_{\rm lr}(t) = \sum_i c_i\, F_{\gamma_i}(t), \qquad
    0 \leq F_{\rm lr}(t) \leq F_{\rm nl}(t) \quad\forall\,t
    \label{eq:lr_mpf}
\end{equation}
and minimize $\|F_{\rm lr}(t) - F_{\rm nl}(t)\|^2$ subject to this causal constraint.

The near-integer coefficients arise because the frontier values $F_{\gamma_i}(t)$

\subsection{Entropy-optimal MPF and two information regimes}
\label{sec:entropy_mpf}

The frontier MPF minimizes squared error in frontier distance — a geometric
proxy that discards amplitude information below threshold.
We propose instead minimizing the \emph{cross-entropy loss}:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{c_i}\; \mathcal{L}(c) = \sum_{v,t} -\ln\frac{n_{\rm MPF}(v,t)}{n_{\rm nl}(v,t)},
    \qquad n_{\rm MPF}(v,t) = \sum_i c_i\, n_{\gamma_i}(v,t)
    \label{eq:entropy_mpf}
\end{equation}
subject to the Lieb-Robinson constraint $0 \leq n_{\rm MPF}(v,t) \leq
n_{\rm nl}(v,t)$ and normalization $\sum_i c_i = 1$.
The gradient is $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial c_k = -\sum_{v,t}
n_{\gamma_k}(v,t)/n_{\rm MPF}(v,t)$, making this a smooth nonlinear
program solved via SLSQP.
Table~\ref{tab:entropy_mpf} summarizes the results; the two topologies
fall into qualitatively different regimes.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Further uses of the wavefront measurement.}
Beyond noise characterization and MPF, the wavefront measurement suggests two
practical directions.
First, \emph{lightcone-aware memory allocation}: the arrival time $t_{\rm nl}(v)$
gives the exact cycle at which each site enters the lightcone; before that cycle
the site tensor is identically $I$ with $\chi=1$, requiring no allocation.
A short pre-flight run measures $v_{\rm hop}$ and provides the per-site entry
schedule, enabling $3$--$13\times$ memory reductions on the expensive large-$\chi$ run.
Second, \emph{BP trust boundary}: belief propagation is exact on trees and first
becomes approximate when the wavefront traverses a loop of length equal to the
graph girth $g$, at cycle $t_{\rm loop} = g/(2v_{\rm hop})$.
For heavy-hex (girth 6): $t_{\rm loop}\approx 8$ cycles;
for rect $7\times7$ (girth 4): $t_{\rm loop}\approx 4.5$ cycles.
This predicts the onset of approximation error directly from the frontier
velocity, without a chi sweep.

\paragraph{Delay vs ratio: are they independent?}
The ratio $r_\gamma(v) \approx \varepsilon_{\rm th} / n_{\rm nl}(v, t_\gamma(v))$
and the delay $l_\gamma(v) = t_\gamma(v) - t_{\rm nl}(v)$ are related through
the noiseless wave growth rate at $v$.
They are not fully independent — both are driven by the same cumulative damping
along the propagation path.
However, $r_\gamma(v)$ additionally encodes the number of gate crossings $N(v)$
along the path, while $l_\gamma(v)$ encodes the threshold-crossing dynamics.
Sites with the same delay but different path lengths will have different ratios,
and vice versa.
A joint $(l_\gamma, r_\gamma)$ distribution is therefore richer than either alone.

\paragraph{Survivorship bias and per-shell statistics.}
The mean delay averaged over all reached sites is biased by which sites the
noisy wave fails to reach — a smaller mean does not imply a faster wave if
the slow distant sites simply dropped out of the sample.
The per-shell entropy loss $\mu_\mathcal{L}(d)$ reported in
Figure~\ref{fig:mpf_entropy} addresses this directly: by grouping sites
by BFS shell distance $d$ from the center, it removes the survivorship bias
and traces how information loss accumulates with propagation distance,
independently of which sites the noisy wave manages to reach.

\paragraph{Noise amplification $\gamma$ as an effective Trotter rescaling.}
The eigenvalue analysis (Table~\ref{tab:eig}) shows that the noise channel
$M_\gamma$ rescales the eigenvalues of the composed PTM without changing its
eigenvectors:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PTM}_{\rm noisy}(\gamma) \approx V \cdot \bigl(\Lambda - \delta\Lambda(\gamma)\bigr) \cdot V^{-1}
\end{equation}
where $V$ are the gate eigenvectors (independent of $\gamma$) and
$\delta\Lambda(\gamma)$ is a small diagonal correction scaling with $\gamma$.
This is structurally identical to reducing the Trotter step:
the ideal gate eigenvalues also scale with $\varepsilon$ as
$\lambda_i(\varepsilon) \approx \lambda_i(0)\cdot g(\varepsilon)$.
Noise acts as a slower effective Trotter step: increasing $\gamma$ reduces
$\varepsilon_{\rm eff}$, so the wave propagates more slowly.
Conversely, noise amplification ($\gamma > 1$) corresponds to an even smaller
$\varepsilon_{\rm eff}$, making the wave slower still — the opposite direction
from varying $\varepsilon$ directly in $\varepsilon$-MPF, where a larger
Trotter step makes the wave faster.
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PTM}_{\rm noisy}(\gamma) \approx \mathrm{PTM}_{\rm ideal}(\varepsilon_{\rm eff}(\gamma)),
    \qquad \varepsilon_{\rm eff}(\gamma) < \varepsilon
\end{equation}
Extrapolating $\gamma \to 0$ recovers $\varepsilon_{\rm eff} \to \varepsilon$,
the ideal gate. Both $\gamma$-MPF and $\varepsilon$-MPF extrapolate back toward
the ideal gate, but from opposite directions: $\varepsilon$-MPF varies the gate
strength directly and monotonically, while $\gamma$-MPF varies it indirectly
through the composed noise-gate product, introducing bond-dependent corrections
that reduce conditioning.

The wavemap delay $l_\gamma(v)$ is a spatial, site-resolved measurement of
this effective rescaling: it records how many extra cycles the noisy wave needs
to reach threshold at each site, relative to the ideal gate.
The site-by-site variation of $l_\gamma(v)$ (Table~\ref{tab:delay}) reflects
the bond-dependent corrections $\delta\Lambda(\gamma)$ — different bonds contribute
different eigenvalue shifts depending on their hardware PTM, making the effective
slowdown spatially non-uniform even for nominally uniform noise amplification.

\paragraph{Connection to MPF and noise learning.}
The Lindblad MPF framework~\cite{robertson2025lmpf} learns per-bond noise
rates from hardware measurements and uses them to construct error-mitigated
observables.
Our wavemap is complementary: rather than learning a noise model, we directly
measure its spatial effect on operator propagation.
The per-site ratio $r_\gamma(v)$ is in principle invertible to per-bond damping
rates along dominant propagation paths — a model-free tomography from wavefront
measurements alone.

\paragraph{Gate structure vs noise structure.}
A key finding is that the Pauli mixing structure of the wave (off-diagonal
PTM fraction $\approx 36\%$) is entirely from the gate and independent of
noise level.
This means the spatial pattern of operator spreading is a property of the
\emph{Hamiltonian}, not the noise.
Noise acts as a transparent filter that delays and damps the gate-determined
wave without redirecting it.
This clean separation validates the wavemap as a noise diagnostic:
the spatial delay and ratio fields encode noise effects on top of a
fixed gate-determined propagation template.

\section*{Future Work}

Several directions remain open.
The wavemap observables $l_\gamma(v)$ and $\mathcal{L}_\gamma(v)$ depend on
the choice of center site; a systematic study across multiple centers would
establish whether the per-shell distributions are universal properties of the
topology and noise model, or depend on the initialization point.
Longer simulations (20+ cycles) would reveal how the wavemap evolves after the
wavefront reflects from the lattice boundary.
On the simulation side, the arrival time $t_{\rm nl}(v)$ provides a per-site
lightcone-aware memory allocation schedule that could reduce tensor network
memory by $3$--$13\times$; and the frontier velocity $v_{\rm hop}$ predicts
the onset of belief propagation approximation error via $t_{\rm loop} = g/(2v_{\rm hop})$,
without requiring an expensive chi sweep.

The most promising longer-term direction is noise classification via machine
learning.
CppSim can generate a labeled training dataset at scale: sweep over a library
of noise models (pure damping, rotation, heterogeneous per-bond, correlated
errors), compute the wavemap for each, and label each simulation with the
noise type and per-bond parameters.
A classifier trained on this dataset learns the map from wavemap signatures
to noise types; a graph neural network operating on the lattice graph is the
natural architecture, as the wavemap is itself a spatial field over the graph.
Deployed on a quantum processor: measure $n(v,t)$ via randomized benchmarking,
compute the wavemap, run the classifier — noise type and severity identified
without further simulation or analytical inversion.
CppSim in this role is a noise characterization laboratory, generating the
ground truth that no experiment alone can provide.
The wavemap observables defined here are the features; the frontier paper is
the foundation.

Finally, $n(v,t)$ is measurable on a quantum processor via randomized
benchmarking or Pauli noise tomography, which would allow direct experimental
validation of the wavemap on hardware and close the loop between classical
simulation and quantum experiment.
